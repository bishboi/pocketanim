Imagine a monastery in the year 1400.
A monk bends over a sheet of parchment, copying a book letter by letter.
How long do you think one Bible took him?
Weeks? Months?
Often well over a year.
So books were rare and costly, kept mostly in churches, universities and palaces.
Then a goldsmith from the German city of Mainz had an idea.
His name was Johannes Gutenberg.
Around 1440, he began working on a way to print books with metal type.
The idea itself was not new.
In China, Bi Sheng had made movable type from baked clay around 1040.
In Korea, a Buddhist book called Jikji was printed with metal type in 1377.
But Gutenberg put together everything Europe needed to print fast.
First, the type.
Each letter was cast in metal, in a hand mould, as a small block called a sort.
The metal was a mix of lead, tin and antimony, which melted easily and set hard.
Thousands of identical sorts could be made, and used again and again.
Second, the ink.
Ink made with water slid off metal, so he used an oily ink, more like paint.
Third, the press.
He borrowed the screw press that farmers used to squeeze grapes and olives.
Here is how a page was made.
A compositor picked the sorts from a case and set them, line by line, into a frame.
The type was inked with leather balls.
A sheet of damp paper was laid on top, and the screw pressed it down.
Pull, lift, and a printed page appeared.
Then the next sheet, and the next.
A team could print hundreds of sheets in a day.
Around 1455, Gutenberg finished his great Bible.
About 180 copies were printed, some on paper, some on vellum.
Fewer than fifty survive today.
Their pages looked like the finest handwriting, but every copy was the same.
Printing needed something else too: cheap paper, and lots of it.
Parchment was made from animal skins, and one Bible on vellum used the skins of about 170 calves.
Paper, made from old linen rags, cost far less.
Paper mills had already spread from Spain and Italy across Europe, ready for the presses.
Gutenberg himself made little money.
He had borrowed heavily from a lender, Johann Fust, and lost his workshop to him in a lawsuit.
But the secret could not be kept.
Printers trained in Mainz carried the craft along Europe's rivers and trade routes.
Presses opened in Cologne, then in Rome, in Venice, in Paris.
In 1476, William Caxton set up England's first press at Westminster.
Printing even crossed the ocean: by 1539, there was a press in Mexico City.
By 1500, there were printing presses in more than two hundred and fifty European towns.
And they had printed something like twenty million books.
Venice became the busiest printing city of all.
There, Aldus Manutius printed small editions of the classics that readers could carry in a pocket.
Books began to change who could read.
As prices fell, merchants, lawyers and craftsmen bought books of their own.
More people learned to read, and more books were written in everyday languages, not only in Latin.
Schools grew, and a teacher could give every pupil the same printed grammar book.
Ideas could now travel faster than ever before.
In 1517, a monk in Wittenberg named Martin Luther wrote ninety-five arguments against the sale of indulgences.
Printers copied them at once.
Within weeks they were being read across Germany.
Luther wrote short pamphlets in German, and printers could hardly keep up.
Printers added woodcut pictures, so even people who could not read could follow the argument.
He became the most printed author of his age.
The church could ban a book, but it could no longer stop it spreading.
Science changed too.
Before printing, every hand copy of a book added new mistakes.
Now a scholar in Italy and a scholar in Poland could study the same page, with the same diagrams and numbers.
In 1543, Nicolaus Copernicus published his book arguing that the Earth moves around the Sun.
Printed copies let others check his work, argue with it, and build on it.
Mistakes could be corrected in the next edition, instead of multiplied.
Maps were printed too.
In 1570, Abraham Ortelius in Antwerp published the first modern atlas, a book of maps of the whole known world.
Sailors, merchants and rulers could all hold the same picture of the world.
Printing also brought the news.
Pamphlets, posters and price lists spread through the towns.
In 1605, a printer in Strasbourg began a weekly paper of news, often called the first newspaper.
So let's compare the two worlds.
Before the press, a book was copied by hand, one at a time, with new mistakes in every copy.
After it, a book was printed by the hundred, every copy the same, at a fraction of the cost.
Knowledge stopped belonging only to the few who held the rare copies.
It belonged to anyone who could read.
A goldsmith's experiment had changed how Europe thought, prayed and argued.
So here is a question to leave you with.
If the printing press could shake the world this much, what is our printing press today?
