Start with a field in a cold, forested Europe.
Between the fifth and tenth centuries, much of the land was covered in woodland, leaving less room for farming.
The climate was also intensely cold.
Winters were long, the growing season short, and harvests poor.
Forests could offer an escape to peasants fleeing harsh treatment.
But from the eleventh century, temperatures rose.
The warmer spell lengthened the growing season and made soil easier to plough.
As forests receded, more land could be brought under cultivation.
Yet a warmer climate alone could not fix the old farming tools.
A wooden plough mostly scratched the surface of the soil.
Fields needed enormous labour, and the crop rotation system used only half the land at a time.
One half grew winter wheat, while the other lay fallow.
The next year, rye went into the fallow half, and the sides switched.
The soil weakened, and famine was common.
Lords wanted more income, so peasants were pushed to cultivate more land and work beyond what they owed.
Did peasants simply accept this pressure?
No.
They quietly kept more of the crop from their own fields, avoided extra unpaid work, and fought over forests and pastures.
Peasants saw these shared resources as useful to the whole community.
Lords increasingly treated them as private property.
Then, by the eleventh century, new tools and farming methods began to alter the balance.
An iron-tipped plough with a mould-board could cut deeper and turn the topsoil.
A shoulder-harness let animals pull with greater force than a neck-harness.
Iron shoes helped protect horses’ feet.
Watermills and windmills could grind corn and press grapes.
The biggest change in field use was the move from two fields to three.
Imagine three strips of land.
One grew wheat or rye for people in autumn.
Another grew peas, beans and lentils in spring, while also providing oats and barley for horses.
The third rested.
Each year, farmers rotated the jobs among the three fields.
More of the land could now produce crops, and the food available from a unit of land doubled.
Beans and peas improved people’s diet and provided fodder for animals.
A peasant could produce more on less land.
The average farm shrank from about 100 acres to 20 or 30 acres by the thirteenth century.
Smaller farms could be cultivated more efficiently, freeing time for other work.
Lords paid for costly mills, while peasants extended arable land, adopted crop rotation and made village smithies to repair tools and horseshoes.
As these changes spread, money began to matter more.
Lords found it convenient to collect cash rent instead of labour.
Cultivators sold crops to traders rather than exchanging them directly for goods.
Prices rose when harvests were poor.
In England, agricultural prices doubled between the 1270s and the 1320s.
More food helped support more people.
Europe’s population rose from about 42 million in 1000 to 62 million around 1200 and 73 million in 1300.
People lived longer than in the eighth century, though women and girls had shorter lifespans than men.
As farmers had grain to sell and needed tools and cloth, marketplaces and periodic fairs grew.
Some became towns with squares, churches, roads, shops and meeting places for local government.
Others grew around castles, bishops’ estates or large churches.
People in towns paid taxes instead of owing labour services to a lord.
For a young person from a peasant family, a town could offer paid work and a chance to escape a lord’s control.
“Town air makes free,” people said.
A serf who stayed hidden in a town for a year and a day without being found by his lord became free.
Towns included escaped serfs, free peasants, labourers, shopkeepers and merchants.
Larger ones also needed people with specialised skills, including bankers and lawyers.
Some reached populations of about 30,000.
Could this growing town population count as a fourth order?
It could.
The guild organised each craft or industry.
Guilds controlled the quality, price and sale of goods, and their leaders met in a guild-hall.
Merchants grew rich as trade expanded.
Scandinavian traders carried furs and hunting-hawks south from the North Sea, while English traders sold tin.
Craft workers who once travelled between manors increasingly settled in towns to make and trade goods.
Their wealth began to rival that of nobles.
Cathedrals also helped towns grow.
From the twelfth century, people contributed labour, materials and money to build large stone churches in France.
Construction took years.
As workers gathered, the surrounding area grew more populated, and the finished cathedral attracted pilgrims.
Inside, stained-glass windows let sunlight fill the space.
Their pictures showed Bible stories to people who could not read.
The building’s shape helped a priest’s voice carry and made the monks’ singing sound beautiful.
Bells could call people to prayer over a great distance.
But expansion brought new pressures.
By the end of the thirteenth century, summers were turning bitterly cold again.
Growing seasons shortened, storms and floods damaged farms, and intensive cultivation exhausted soil.
Less pasture meant fewer cattle, while population had grown faster than resources.
Famines struck in 1315 to 1317, followed by widespread cattle deaths.
A shortage of silver also forced governments to reduce the precious metal in their coins.
Then came the Black Death.
Ships carrying goods from distant places also carried rats infected with bubonic plague.
Between 1347 and 1350, about one-fifth of Europe’s population died, and some places lost as much as two-fifths.
Cities and enclosed communities were especially vulnerable.
Europe’s population fell from 73 million in 1300 to 45 million in 1400.
With fewer workers, wages rose, while the prices of farm goods fell.
Lords tried to revive labour services instead of the money agreements they had made.
Peasants resisted, and revolts erupted in Flanders, France and England.
The rebellions were crushed, but the old feudal ties could not simply be restored.
So if land and labour had weakened the old order, what kind of power would take its place?
